## The Big Idea

A feature answers the request in front of you. A capability answers the requests you haven't gotten yet. Knowing which to build is a skill - and most of us learn it the hard way.

## Highlight this week's on-site visits

At [Craftwork](https://craftwork.com), we visit some sales leads in person before we quote their project. It's a great way for us to understand their needs, and for them to understand our process.

I'd just built a map view of our leads, and the team was using it. Then a request came in: could the map highlight leads with on-site visits _this week_?

Clear, specific, and worth building. But I could already hear the next request: _this week and next?_ Then: _on-sites, plus good candidates for next week?_

The request wasn't wrong. People describe the pain in front of them. Designing the system underneath is my team's role.

## When your endpoint name reads like a sentence

Building exactly what was asked would have looked like this:

```tsx
const leads = api
  .v1
  .leads
  .highlight
  .onSiteVisitsForNextWeek();
```

You probably winced. When a name reads like a sentence, the parameters are baked in. Every variation is a new endpoint, a new ticket, a new deploy.

So I built something else. Our list views already had filters: give them rules, get matching leads. I added a `highlight` endpoint that uses the same set of rules, and returns highlights instead.

The team pushed back - generic _sounds_ like it'd take longer to build. I built it anyway. Call it quiet rebellion.

Like I said, because the list view was already working, it was a pretty quick feature to build. The filters already existed, and highlighting was a new way to use them.

**Capabilities compound. Each one makes the next one cheaper.**

## Ship the feature request, then show what else it can do

Once highlighting was ready to ship, I wanted to show the team what it could do.

I used a little [reverse devrel](https://mikebifulco.com/newsletter/reverse-devrel-as-core-engineering-skill), and shared a short video showing how to highlight leads for next week. Then, _by the way_ - the same menu highlights every exterior project. Or everything assigned to a specific account manager. Or everything over $10,000.

It looked just like the filters they already knew, so there was nothing to learn. Lead with the request, then show the permutations, and make sure it finds its audience.

A capability nobody discovers is just a feature with extra steps.

## Apple built Do Not Disturb four times

Do Not Disturb on iOS is a great example of a feature which got rebuilt a handful of times before it became a full-blown capability.

This is how DND has evolved since its introduction:

- **2012:** iOS 6 introduces Do Not Disturb.
- **2017:** iOS 11 adds Do Not Disturb While Driving.
- **2018:** iOS 12 adds Bedtime mode.
- **2021:** iOS 15 replaces them all with Focus: build your own modes, with your own rules. The old ones become presets.

Each special case was reasonable. Together, they were a capability waiting to happen. Apple can afford nine years of special cases. Your startup can't.

## How to spot a capability hiding in a feature request

How did I know the map request would creep? It's something you grow a sixth sense for over time.

I've built the specific version before, then found ten more versions waiting behind it, each requiring engineering time to implement. Do that enough, and it becomes an immune response.

No scars yet? Look for two tells:

- **High specificity.** `onSiteVisitsForNextWeek()` bakes in three decisions: a time range, a visit type, a status. A change in any of these would become a future request.
- **You can't finish the list.** Sales statuses: new, investigating, waiting for feedback, verbal offer, sold... Are you _sure_ that's all? If you can't write the complete list, don't hardcode it. Build the thing that holds the list.

You don't have to build the whole generic system on day one. You just have to see what it would look like - and not paint yourself into a corner.

## When the simple version is the right call

This practice is in tension with something I believe just as strongly: [build just enough](https://mikebifulco.com/newsletter/just-enough-is-an-ongoing-practice). The tiebreaker isn't _will this change?_ It's _what will the change cost?_

- When **Growth is Additive**, start small. My personal health app started with weight and BMI. Resting heart rate and VO2 max snapped in later. Nothing got rebuilt.
- If **N=1 user**, keep it simple. This newsletter goes out through Resend's _Broadcast_ feature. Instead of building an editor, a CI action drafts the broadcast from markdown, and I send it from Resend. I'm the only end user of this workflow. The best capability is sometimes one someone else built.
- If you can't predict all of the use cases for a feature (like how and why people might try to filter a list), aim for flexibility.

## Focus mode for your engineering team

Configurable-first costs more up front and pays off quietly.

When a capability is good, people use it constantly without noticing. Nobody files a ticket. Nobody pings an engineer. That's the point - and why it's easy to forget to [celebrate](https://mikebifulco.com/newsletter/mvp-event-horizon).

We're still building this muscle at Craftwork. You build it through repetition, and by looking back at what you shipped and asking what it should have been.

The reward is the one Apple eventually figured out: when the system handles the variations, your engineers get their own Focus mode.

## What the operator problem asks of you

The operator problem starts the moment people depend on something you built. This series covered four shifts:

1. [Rebuild without stopping the business](https://mikebifulco.com/newsletter/stangler-fig-pattern). When your prototype becomes the product, you can't just start over.
2. [After the MVP, the work changes](https://mikebifulco.com/newsletter/operator-problem-after-mvp). Builder and operator are different jobs.
3. [Every MVP has an event horizon](https://mikebifulco.com/newsletter/mvp-event-horizon). Know which mode each system needs.
4. Features become capabilities. Stop building the same button twice.

None of it is as visible as a launch. But it's the work that turns an experiment into a dependable company.

Operator problems are good problems. They mean something you built mattered.
